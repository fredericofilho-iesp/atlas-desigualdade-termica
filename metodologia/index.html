Principles

  1. Land surface temperature (LST, from satellites) and air temperature (stations or reanalysis) are distinct physical concepts and appear as separate indicators.
  2. For each indicator we record unit, period, original resolution, treatment of missing values and spatial aggregation method.
  3. No simulated data is presented as an observation.
  4. Cross-country comparisons use only internationally harmonised products; national data appears as a complementary layer, with differences in definitions documented.
  5. Area-level associations (ecological fallacy) are not interpreted as individual or causal relationships.

Summer land surface temperature (city of Rio)

Data: Landsat 8 and 9, Collection 2 Level-2 surface temperature band (USGS), accessed through Microsoft Planetary Computer. All scenes from 1 December to 31 March in the summers of 2022/23, 2023/24 and 2024/25 with scene cloud cover below 70% (27 scenes, WRS-2 path 217, row 76). Overpass at about 10 a.m. local time.

Processing: pixels flagged as fill, cloud, dilated cloud, cirrus, cloud shadow or water in the QA_PIXEL band are removed. For each 30 m pixel we take the median of all valid observations, requiring at least 3. Each census tract receives the area-weighted median of the pixels it covers; tracts with less than half a valid pixel are left without data.

Difference from the city median: tract value minus the median of all tracts (41.8 °C). Fixed classes: below −4, −4 to −1.5, −1.5 to 1.5, 1.5 to 4, above 4 °C.

Limits: land surface temperature measures how hot surfaces (roofs, pavement, vegetation) get, not the air temperature people breathe, and only on clear mornings. It is a good indicator of the spatial pattern of urban heat, not of thermal comfort or health risk on its own. The script, the list of scenes and the 30 m raster are in the repository.

Household-head income (city of Rio)

Data: IBGE, 2022 Census, universe results, Census tract aggregates – Income of the household head (release of May 2026). Variables V06004 (mean) and V06006 (median) monthly nominal income of household heads with income in occupied private permanent households, and V06001 (number of household heads), used as weight. Values in nominal R$ of July 2022 (1 minimum wage = R$ 1,212). 13,368 of 13,782 tracts have data; the rest have no households or were suppressed by IBGE for confidentiality.

Limits: this is not per capita household income. Heads without income are excluded from the mean, so tracts with many such households look better off than they are.

Heat × income

Income fifths: tracts are ordered by mean household-head income and grouped so that each fifth holds about 20% of the city’s households (≈487,000). For each fifth we compute the household-weighted mean summer surface temperature. The 95% confidence interval of the difference between the poorest and richest fifths comes from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of tracts.

Result (2022/23–2024/25 summers): poorest fifth 41.6 °C, second 41.9 °C, third 42.7 °C, fourth 42.2 °C, richest 38.5 °C. Difference poorest − richest: 3.0 °C (95% CI 2.9 to 3.2). The relationship is not linear: the hottest surfaces are in middle-income tracts, and the main divide separates the richest fifth from the rest of the city. Part of the poorest fifth lives in hillside favelas next to forest and in less dense areas of the West Zone, where surfaces are cooler.

Bivariate map: household-weighted terciles of temperature (limits 40.5 and 43.0 °C) and of income (R$ 2,000 and R$ 3,900), combined into 9 classes.

Reading: these are associations between areas (ecological fallacy). They do not show that each low-income household is more exposed, nor that income causes temperature.

Street trees (city of Rio)

Data: IBGE, 2022 Census, urban characteristics of the surroundings of households, residents file. Variables V05230–V05233: residents of occupied private households, in tracts selected for the surroundings survey, by the number of trees on their block face (none, 1–2, 3–4, 5 or more), as observed by census enumerators. Block faces not observed (V05234, 0.9% of residents) are excluded. 13,235 tracts have data (at least 20 residents observed); tracts outside the survey, mostly non-urban, have none.

Indicators: share of residents on block faces with at least one tree, and with 5 or more trees. City-wide: 64.5% and 37.9%.

Result: by income fifth, the share of residents on blocks with trees rises from 26% (poorest) to 51%, 74%, 84% and 95% (richest); with 5+ trees, from 8% to 79%. Tracts in the most-treed fifth have summer surfaces about 1.9 °C cooler than those in the least-treed fifth.

Limits: this is a count of trees on the street in front of the home, not canopy cover, and does not include parks, forests or backyards. In favelas, block faces are often narrow alleys where street trees are rare even close to forest.

Age and color or race (city of Rio)

Data: IBGE, 2022 Census, census tract aggregates (universe), demography and color or race files. Variables V01006 (residents), V01031 (aged 0–4), V01040 + V01041 (aged 60–69 and 70+), V01318 (preta) and V01320 (parda). The census does not separate 65+ in this file; we use 60+, the legal definition of older person in Brazil. Color or race is self-declared using IBGE categories; preta and parda together form the Black population in Brazilian statistics.

Indicators: share of residents aged 60+, aged 0–4, and preta or parda. City-wide: 20.2%, 5.0% and 54.4% (white: 45.3%). Tracts with fewer than 20 residents have no data. IBGE suppresses small counts for confidentiality; this mostly affects the yellow (Asian-descendant) and Indigenous groups, which are counted as zero where suppressed and are not shown separately.

Exposure by group: the household-weighting used for income is replaced by the number of people of each group in each tract. Result: white residents 41.0 °C and 73.5% on blocks with trees; preta 41.9 °C and 55.3%; parda 41.8 °C and 57.3%. Black and brown − white: 0.83 °C (95% CI 0.78 to 0.88), 16.8 percentage points fewer on treed blocks. Older adults (41.4 °C, 72.8%) are not more exposed than the average resident, because they are concentrated in older, wealthier neighborhoods; children 0–4 are slightly more exposed (41.5 °C, 57.7%).

Map classification

By default each indicator is split into five quantile classes, computed in R at publication time. The method and class limits are shown in the map legend.
