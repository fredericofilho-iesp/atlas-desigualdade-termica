Principles

  1. Land surface temperature (LST, from satellites) and air temperature (stations or reanalysis) are distinct physical concepts and appear as separate indicators.
  2. For each indicator we record unit, period, original resolution, treatment of missing values and spatial aggregation method.
  3. No simulated data is presented as an observation.
  4. Cross-country comparisons use only internationally harmonised products; national data appears as a complementary layer, with differences in definitions documented.
  5. Area-level associations (ecological fallacy) are not interpreted as individual or causal relationships.

Summer land surface temperature (city of Rio)

Data: Landsat 8 and 9, Collection 2 Level-2 surface temperature band (USGS), accessed through Microsoft Planetary Computer. All scenes from 1 December to 31 March in the summers of 2022/23, 2023/24 and 2024/25 with scene cloud cover below 70% (27 scenes, WRS-2 path 217, row 76). Overpass at about 10 a.m. local time.

Processing: pixels flagged as fill, cloud, dilated cloud, cirrus, cloud shadow or water in the QA_PIXEL band are removed. For each 30 m pixel we take the median of all valid observations, requiring at least 3. Each census tract receives the area-weighted median of the pixels it covers; tracts with less than half a valid pixel are left without data.

Difference from the city median: tract value minus the median of all tracts (41.8 °C). Fixed classes: below −4, −4 to −1.5, −1.5 to 1.5, 1.5 to 4, above 4 °C.

Limits: land surface temperature measures how hot surfaces (roofs, pavement, vegetation) get, not the air temperature people breathe, and only on clear mornings. It is a good indicator of the spatial pattern of urban heat, not of thermal comfort or health risk on its own. The script, the list of scenes and the 30 m raster are in the repository.

Household-head income (city of Rio)

Data: IBGE, 2022 Census, universe results, Census tract aggregates – Income of the household head (release of May 2026). Variables V06004 (mean) and V06006 (median) monthly nominal income of household heads with income in occupied private permanent households, and V06001 (number of household heads), used as weight. Values in nominal R$ of July 2022 (1 minimum wage = R$ 1,212). 13,368 of 13,782 tracts have data; the rest have no households or were suppressed by IBGE for confidentiality.

Limits: this is not per capita household income. Heads without income are excluded from the mean, so tracts with many such households look better off than they are.

Heat × income

Income fifths: tracts are ordered by mean household-head income and grouped so that each fifth holds about 20% of the city’s households (≈487,000). For each fifth we compute the household-weighted mean summer surface temperature. The 95% confidence interval of the difference between the poorest and richest fifths comes from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of tracts.

Result (2022/23–2024/25 summers): poorest fifth 41.6 °C, second 41.9 °C, third 42.7 °C, fourth 42.2 °C, richest 38.5 °C. Difference poorest − richest: 3.0 °C (95% CI 2.9 to 3.2). The relationship is not linear: the hottest surfaces are in middle-income tracts, and the main divide separates the richest fifth from the rest of the city. Part of the poorest fifth lives in hillside favelas next to forest and in less dense areas of the West Zone, where surfaces are cooler.

Bivariate map: household-weighted terciles of temperature (limits 40.5 and 43.0 °C) and of income (R$ 2,000 and R$ 3,900), combined into 9 classes.

Reading: these are associations between areas (ecological fallacy). They do not show that each low-income household is more exposed, nor that income causes temperature.

Street trees (city of Rio)

Data: IBGE, 2022 Census, urban characteristics of the surroundings of households, residents file. Variables V05230–V05233: residents of occupied private households, in tracts selected for the surroundings survey, by the number of trees on their block face (none, 1–2, 3–4, 5 or more), as observed by census enumerators. Block faces not observed (V05234, 0.9% of residents) are excluded. 13,235 tracts have data (at least 20 residents observed); tracts outside the survey, mostly non-urban, have none.

Indicators: share of residents on block faces with at least one tree, and with 5 or more trees. City-wide: 64.5% and 37.9%.

Result: by income fifth, the share of residents on blocks with trees rises from 26% (poorest) to 51%, 74%, 84% and 95% (richest); with 5+ trees, from 8% to 79%. Tracts in the most-treed fifth have summer surfaces about 1.9 °C cooler than those in the least-treed fifth.

Limits: this is a count of trees on the street in front of the home, not canopy cover, and does not include parks, forests or backyards. In favelas, block faces are often narrow alleys where street trees are rare even close to forest.

Age and color or race (city of Rio)

Data: IBGE, 2022 Census, census tract aggregates (universe), demography and color or race files. Variables V01006 (residents), V01031 (aged 0–4), V01040 + V01041 (aged 60–69 and 70+), V01318 (preta) and V01320 (parda). The census does not separate 65+ in this file; we use 60+, the legal definition of older person in Brazil. Color or race is self-declared using IBGE categories; preta and parda together form the Black population in Brazilian statistics.

Indicators: share of residents aged 60+, aged 0–4, and preta or parda. City-wide: 20.2%, 5.0% and 54.4% (white: 45.3%). Tracts with fewer than 20 residents have no data. IBGE suppresses small counts for confidentiality; this mostly affects the yellow (Asian-descendant) and Indigenous groups, which are counted as zero where suppressed and are not shown separately.

Exposure by group: the household-weighting used for income is replaced by the number of people of each group in each tract. Result: white residents 41.0 °C and 73.5% on blocks with trees; preta 41.9 °C and 55.3%; parda 41.8 °C and 57.3%. Black and brown − white: 0.83 °C (95% CI 0.78 to 0.88), 16.8 percentage points fewer on treed blocks. Older adults (41.4 °C, 72.8%) are not more exposed than the average resident, because they are concentrated in older, wealthier neighborhoods; children 0–4 are slightly more exposed (41.5 °C, 57.7%).

Rio de Janeiro State

The state map shows the 92 municipalities, but every value is built from the 2022 census tracts (about 41,700 tracts in the IBGE boundary file). This keeps the state scale consistent with the city of Rio and measures heat where people live: a municipality with large forested or rural areas is not made to look cooler simply because most of its area is uninhabited.

Surface temperature. Same imagery, summers and cloud mask as the city of Rio (Landsat 8–9, December–March, 2022/23 to 2024/25), but summarised scene by scene: for each scene, the mean of clear pixels in each tract (weighted by the share of each pixel inside the tract), used only when at least half of the tract is clear; scenes from the same date are averaged, and the tract value is the median across dates (at least 3). This avoids building a per-pixel composite across ten Landsat tiles and two UTM zones. In the 13,733 tracts of the city of Rio, the two methods agree closely (correlation 0.994; mean difference 0.02 °C).

Municipal values. Temperature is the mean of tract values weighted by residents; income is weighted by household heads; shares (Black and brown population, older adults, residents on treed blocks) are summed over tracts before dividing. The “difference from state” indicator subtracts the state value (all tracts, weighted by residents).

Inequality within each municipality. Poorest − richest: surface temperature of the poorest fifth of households minus the richest fifth, with fifths formed from that municipality’s own tracts (household-weighted); only municipalities with at least 30 tracts with data. Black and brown − white: group-weighted mean temperature difference, only where each group has at least 2,000 people in tracts with data. Municipalities below these thresholds appear without a value rather than with an unstable estimate. The panels below the map summarise all tracts in the state, with 95% bootstrap intervals (500 replicates).

Brazil

The national map shows the 5,570 municipalities, with values built from the 458,772 tracts of the 2022 Census and weighted by people, as in the state map.

Surface temperature (MODIS). Landsat for the whole country would take some 12,000 scenes, so this scale uses MODIS/Aqua MYD11A2 v6.1 (8-day composites, 1 km, early-afternoon overpass, close to the daily maximum), 2022–2024, from Microsoft Planetary Computer. Summer (December–March) is not the hottest time everywhere: in much of the Centre-West and North the peak comes in the dry season (August–October), and the rainy summer is cloudy. So, for each pixel, the monthly means of the three years are computed, then the averages of three consecutive months, and the highest of these is kept: the hottest three months of each place. Each tract takes the mean of the pixels it overlaps. The hottest season is centred on September–October for most tracts in the North and Centre-West and on January for most in the South and Southeast. In Rio de Janeiro State, tract values from MODIS and from Landsat are well correlated (0.79), with MODIS about 2.2 °C lower because 1 km pixels average hot and cool surfaces.

Comparing within the same state. Across the whole country, regional climate is mixed with social inequality: the South is richer, whiter and cooler, and the interior of the Northeast poorer and hotter. The national panels therefore also report comparisons within each state, with temperature expressed as the difference from the state average and income fifths formed inside each state. The two answer different questions: the national figure describes who lives in the hottest parts of Brazil; the within-state figure describes inequality between neighbours who share a climate. At 1 km, within-state results also reflect the contrast between towns and their rural surroundings rather than between neighbourhoods; the city and state maps, with Landsat at 30 m, are the reference for intra-urban inequality.

Latin America

The regional map covers the 33 countries of Latin America and the Caribbean (CELAC) by first-level subnational unit (states, provinces, departments, regions), with boundaries from geoBoundaries. There is no census comparable across countries, so every value is built from harmonised global grids: each populated ~1 km cell of GHSL GHS-POP 2020 (European Commission, JRC) receives the MODIS temperature of the pixel it falls in, and unit and country values are means weighted by population.

Surface temperature (MODIS). Same method as the Brazil map: MODIS/Aqua MYD11A2 v6.1, 2022–2024, the hottest three consecutive months of each pixel. The hottest season is centred on April–May in Mexico and Central America, on January in the Southern Cone and southern Brazil and on the dry season (September–October) in the Amazon and the Bolivian lowlands. In cities repeats the calculation only for dense urban cells (at least 1,500 inhabitants per km², the GHSL urban-centre threshold); urban population uses the GHSL urban-cluster threshold (300 per km²).

Inequality by income, within cities. There is no neighbourhood income measure comparable across countries, so each country uses its best official socioeconomic measure for small urban areas: household-head income by census tract in Brazil (IBGE, 2022 Census; urban tracts), the urban marginalisation index by urban AGEB in Mexico (CONAPO 2020, with INEGI boundaries) and multidimensional poverty by urban block in Colombia (DANE, 2018 Census). Each small area receives the MODIS temperature (mean of the pixels it covers in Mexico; the pixel at its centre for the Colombian blocks; tract means already computed for the Brazil map). Areas are ranked into fifths within their own municipality, weighted by population, and we compare each area’s deviation from its municipal mean between the poorest and the richest fifth. Comparing within municipalities keeps regional climate out of the comparison: ranked nationally, Colombia’s poorest look 2 °C hotter and Mexico’s 2 °C cooler, mostly because of where poorer regions are. Only municipalities with at least 20 small areas enter; country intervals resample municipalities. In the city of Rio de Janeiro the method gives +3.3 °C, close to the Landsat 30 m result (+3.0 °C). MODIS pixels are 1 km, so inequality between adjacent blocks is smoothed; and the three measures are not the same thing, so directions are more comparable than exact values.

A wealth map we tested and rejected. The only wealth map covering the whole region at neighbourhood scale is Meta’s Relative Wealth Index (RWI, ~2.4 km blocks, estimated from satellite imagery and connectivity data). We tested it where the census gives the answer. In the city of Rio de Janeiro, across 180 blocks, the RWI barely agreed with the household income of the 2022 Census (rank correlation 0.16) and ranked the dense North Zone as the richest area. In the same blocks, census income and MODIS temperature agreed with the Landsat result (poorer places are hotter; MODIS and Landsat correlate at 0.83). With the RWI, the sign flipped: in none of the 19 countries with data did the “poorest” live in hotter places, and in most they came out in cooler ones, including Rio de Janeiro State, where census data show the poorest fifth 3.0 °C hotter. The RWI seems to capture how built-up an area is more than its income, and built-up areas are hotter. We therefore did not publish it. That is why income comparisons at this scale use national census data, country by country.

Extreme heat days (air temperature)

For Rio de Janeiro State, the atlas also shows air temperature, which is what the body feels and what heat alerts use. The source is ERA5-Land (ECMWF/Copernicus), a reanalysis on a 0.1° grid (~11 km), read from the public copy kept by Open-Meteo on AWS Open Data (no account needed; CC BY 4.0). From hourly 2 m temperature we take the maximum of each civil day (UTC−3), 2016–2025. Each census tract takes the grid cell it falls in (coastal tracts in cells counted as sea take the nearest land cell: about one in five tracts, mostly around Guanabara Bay) and municipal values are weighted by residents. Extreme heat days: mean number of days per year with a maximum of 35 °C or more; summer maximum: mean daily maximum from December to March. An 11 km grid cannot see neighbourhood heat islands and tends to underestimate maxima in dense urban areas, so it compares municipalities, not streets. Across municipalities, the summer maximum agrees with the Landsat surface temperature (r = 0.72), and the Baixada Fluminense comes out hottest.

Bivariate maps

The bivariate maps cross surface temperature with income, with the Black (preta and parda) share of residents and, at the national scale, with the Indigenous share. Each axis is split into thirds: in the city of Rio, thirds of households (income) or residents (color or race); in the state and national maps, thirds of municipalities, because weighting by population would let a single capital fill almost one third on its own. The magenta corner always marks the most unfavourable combination: hotter and poorer, or hotter and with more residents of the group (the group axes therefore run from more to fewer). In the national maps the temperature axis is the difference from the state average, so that regional climate does not pass for inequality. The Indigenous share (IBGE, 2022 Census, SIDRA table 9605, color or race) uses fixed classes (under 1%, 1–10%, 10% or more), because most municipalities are below 1%; it is not mapped for the city or state of Rio de Janeiro, where Indigenous people are about 0.1% of residents. The maps show where combinations occur; the inequality indicators and panels measure them.

Map classification

By default each indicator is split into five quantile classes, computed in R at publication time. The method and class limits are shown in the map legend.
